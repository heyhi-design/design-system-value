# Executive deck skeleton

Three occasions, three decks. All three follow the executive-summary grammar that finance and strategy audiences already read fluently (the Total Economic Impact shape: headline metrics, benefits by category with method, unquantified benefits named, costs, a synopsis, then the story), adapted to the honesty rules of the field guide. Every slide names its data source; every number links to its definition.

Each deck below is a slide plan, not a built deck. Building one runs the five stages (classify the source and audience, extract the argument, select the structure, map the data viz, write the plan) and passes the ghost-deck test before any slide is drawn: read the action titles in order and they carry the whole argument on their own. Action titles are complete sentences that state the finding or the ask, not topic labels, so each title is written from the organization's real numbers at build time. This template holds the slot, not the sentence.

Three calls stay the team's judgment and are left blank here by construction: which metric leads (the headline), the recommendation or decision, and the ask. The template names each as an empty slot; a real deck fills it from the store and the team's own call, never from an invented figure.

Slide counts are ceilings; cut before adding.

## Deck A. First funding (8–10 slides)

Audience: whoever owns the budget line the system would sit in. Goal: a bounded yes.
Structure: pitch grammar (problem, proof on a few teams, the system as a product, a specific ask), carried on the executive-summary shape.

1. **The problem in one picture.** The interface inventory: a wall of the 37 button styles (or the equivalent). No text beyond a caption with the count and the date.
2. **What it costs us today.** Avoided-duplication arithmetic (M4 method d, from the duplication ledger) with the organization's own numbers: teams × hours × loaded rate for the last three shared patterns built more than once. Sources named. A range, not a point.
3. **What we already proved.** The traction slide: two or three teams, what they shipped on the shared components, hours or defects, a quote from each. If a small task study exists, its number here with n stated.
4. **What we propose.** The system as a product: consumers, first release scope, roadmap for two quarters.
5. **What it will cost.** Ledger 1: people, tooling, maintenance reserve, per year, with the support ratio.
6. **How we will know.** The three metrics we will publish, their definitions, the baseline date, and the first target date. The one-page report shown as a mock with real baseline numbers.
7. **Risk it reduces.** One: accessibility exposure, brand inconsistency, or key-person dependency, in the organization's terms.
8. **The ask.** Headcount or budget, for a stated period, with a stated stopping point and the date of the first read-out.
9. *(Appendix)* Method notes, provenance of any borrowed benchmark, the metric definitions.

## Deck B. Renewal or annual review (10–12 slides)

Audience: the sponsor and their peers. Goal: continued or expanded investment, and targets for next year.
Structure: strategic grammar (trajectory and trend, this year's proofs, next-year direction, the ask), on the executive-summary shape.

1. **Headline with trend.** One number, baseline → now → target, six to eight periods.
2. **Scorecard.** The one-pager's scorecard row, cost first: cost per consuming team, override rate, defects on versus off system, engagement composite. Coverage is the headline slide before it.
3. **The migration chart.** System line up, legacy line down, for this year's named migration.
4. **Where it is and is not.** Team view excerpt: top three and bottom three teams by coverage with their engagement quadrant.
5. **Proof story one.** Event card: what happened, cost last time, cost this time, before/after, quote.
6. **Proof story two.** A second event or the task study.
7. **Quality and risk.** Defects on-system versus off-system; accessibility posture trend.
8. **What we are not counting.** Named unquantified benefits.
9. **What it cost.** Ledger 1 actuals versus plan; support ratio; cost per consumer trend.
10. **What consumers say.** Satisfaction tiles with response rate; three verbatims.
11. **Next year.** Product roadmap, the two or three targets, the one metric we will add and the one we will retire.
12. **The ask.** Hold, expand, mandate: the specific decision.

## Deck C. Defense against cuts (6–8 slides)

Audience: whoever is deciding. Goal: a specific, informed decision rather than a default cut.
Structure: strategic grammar closing on a decision with options, on the executive-summary shape.

1. **What runs on this.** The platform slide: N products, M teams, coverage per product and platform from the store; if the analytics tool can attribute traffic to system-built surfaces (the IBM.com page-view pattern), that share too.
2. **What stops if the team stops.** Release cadence, contribution SLA, the open migration, the queue of consuming-team requests, dated.
3. **What it costs versus what it avoids.** Ledger 1 (cost) beside the avoided-duplication ledger (M4 method d) and the two strongest event cards (artifact 07, part 4).
4. **Leading indicators now.** Engagement and sponsor metrics: is the relationship healthy or already fraying; the quadrant.
5. **Risk on the table.** Accessibility exposure and key-person dependency, stated plainly, with what a re-formed team would cost later.
6. **Options.** Three: hold at current size, reduce to a stated minimum with what is dropped, or stop with the shutdown plan and its cost. The recommendation is stated as a decision; it is the owner's call and stays blank in this template.
7. *(Appendix)* Definitions, sources, the full team view.

## Slide rules

- **One idea per slide, one number per idea, the source in small type at the bottom.**
- **Action titles are complete sentences.** Each title states the finding or the ask ("Coverage of what users see rose from X to Y in N months"), not a topic label ("Coverage"). Read the titles in order and they carry the argument alone; the specific sentence is written from the organization's numbers at build time.
- **Two dates beat one.** Any chart with a single period gets replaced by a two-period comparison (the IBM.com two-bar pattern).
- **No gauges, no multi-slice donuts, no unsourced benchmark cards.** Rings for a bounded percentage, sparklines for trends, bars for comparisons, a treemap for composition. Every chart annotates its key point directly on it (a labeled bar, a callout, an arrow), so the audience never hunts for it.
- **Quotes sit beside numbers,** never on their own slide.
- **Every number carries its provenance.** A borrowed benchmark keeps the field-guide grade and provenance state it arrived with; nothing graded X or unsourced reaches a slide, and no correlational design-ROI figure is shown as a headline number. The organization's own numbers name the store table they were queried from.
- **Language:** consistency becomes trust; efficiency becomes capacity and says what the capacity shipped; "done" becomes a product with a roadmap; accessibility becomes risk.
- **Voice, hard rules.** Zero em dashes; plain, concrete words over marketing abstractions; describe the function, not the label.
- **Generate from the store.** Every chart is a query against the tables in artifact 02, exported on the report date, so the slide never disagrees with the dashboard.
